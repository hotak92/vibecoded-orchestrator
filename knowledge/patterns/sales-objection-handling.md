# Sales Objection Handling Library

A practical library of the most common B2B sales objections, the trigger phrase to listen for, the response pattern that doesn't sound defensive, and the follow-up question that keeps the conversation alive. Use this as a cheat sheet on calls and in reply branches of cold outbound sequences. Adapt language to the vendor's voice — the patterns are the bones, the words are the flesh.

## The universal pattern

Across all objection types, the response shape is the same three steps:

1. **Acknowledge** without defending — "Got it." / "Makes sense." / "Totally fair."
2. **Reframe** with a perspective the prospect hasn't considered
3. **Follow-up question** that keeps the conversation alive (don't let them close the loop on "no")

A defensive response ("but actually our product...") signals weakness and amplifies the objection. The acknowledge-reframe-question pattern signals confidence and turns the objection into discovery.

## The eight most common objections

### 1. "Too expensive"

**Trigger phrases**: "out of our budget", "more than we wanted to spend", "let me check with finance", price reaction in body language on screen-share.

**Response pattern**: Don't justify the price. Reframe to ROI or cost-of-inaction.

> "Got it — our price is $X. The way I think about it: what's it costing you NOT to solve [pain]? When teams don't fix [pain], the typical cost is [specific outcome — missed forecast, churn risk, ops burnout]. What's that worth at your stage?"

**Follow-up question**: "What's the budget envelope for [category] tooling this year?" — surfaces real number; reveals whether this is "price too high" or "category not budgeted."

### 2. "Already using [Competitor X]"

**Trigger phrases**: "we have [Tool]", "switched to [Vendor] last year", "happy with what we have".

**Response pattern**: Don't bash the competitor. Ask what's working and what isn't.

> "Smart choice — [Competitor] is solid at [their strength]. Curious: what's NOT working with the current setup? Most teams I talk to who use [Competitor] hit a wall at [specific limitation we cover]."

**Follow-up question**: "When was the last time the [Competitor] report matched the [other data source] exactly?" — surfaces the specific data quality / coverage / scale issue you typically solve.

If genuinely happy: "Got it — we can be additive rather than a replacement. Interested in [specific complementary use case]?"

### 3. "Bad timing — not now"

**Trigger phrases**: "we'll revisit in Q3", "next year maybe", "right now we're focused on [other thing]".

**Response pattern**: Don't fight the timing. Anchor a specific re-touch date.

> "Makes sense. So — Q3 or Q4? I'll put a placeholder in my calendar and ping you then. Want me to share [relevant asset] in the meantime so it's top of mind when timing's right?"

**Follow-up question**: "What would need to be true for this to become urgent before [their stated timeframe]?" — sometimes reveals a trigger event you can monitor.

### 4. "Need to discuss with the team"

**Trigger phrases**: "loop in my boss", "need buy-in from [role]", "let me run this past [person]".

**Response pattern**: Offer to help build the case. Multi-thread the deal.

> "Totally fair — these decisions involve more than one person. Two thoughts: (1) Who else would be in the room? Happy to set up a call directly with [them] + you, saves a round-trip. (2) Want me to put together a 1-page summary you can forward — happy to make it specific to [their role]?"

**Follow-up question**: "Who else typically has a say in [category] decisions at [Company]?" — surfaces the buying committee even if they hadn't volunteered names.

### 5. "Send me materials, I'll review"

**Trigger phrases**: "send a one-pager", "I'll review and get back", "do you have a deck?"

**Response pattern**: Counter with screen-share or live-fit-check.

> "Will do — but I find a 15-min screen-share answers things 10x faster than a PDF does. Want to do that instead? Or I can send the materials AND book the 15-min — your call."

**Follow-up question**: "What specifically would help you decide? — most things are easier to show than describe." — surfaces the actual decision criterion vs the polite-no signal.

### 6. "We built this in-house" / "We can build it ourselves"

**Trigger phrases**: "our eng team is already on it", "internal tool", "we have a script for that".

**Response pattern**: Acknowledge the capability. Reframe to opportunity cost.

> "Most teams I talk to could build this — the question is opportunity cost. Your eng team would take [X weeks]; that's [Y feature/sprint of product] not shipping. We do it in [Z time]. Is the eng team's roadmap pressure high enough that [Y] would be missed?"

**Follow-up question**: "What's the engineering team's roadmap pressure look like right now?" — surfaces whether DIY is realistic or a polite deflection.

### 7. "Just researching"

**Trigger phrases**: "early stages", "comparing options", "no urgency yet".

**Response pattern**: Qualify — distinguish real research from polite no.

> "Totally fine — what would make this worth a follow-up in 30 days? What would change in your world?"

**Follow-up question**: "What's prompting the research? Is something not working with the current setup, or is this more 'understand the market'?" — distinguishes pain-driven research (high intent) from category-education research (low intent).

### 8. "Not interested" / unsubscribe

**Trigger phrases**: "no thanks", "remove me from your list", "stop emailing me".

**Response pattern**: Honour immediately. Single-line acknowledgement.

> "All good — taking you off the list. If anything changes, you know where to find me."

**Follow-up question**: None. Respect the no. Suppress permanently in the CRM. (Continuing to contact someone who said "remove me" is illegal under CAN-SPAM, GDPR, and CASL.)

## Niche but high-stakes objections

### "We need a custom proposal" (without budget signal)

This is often fishing for ideas. Counter with discovery, not proposal-building:

> "Happy to scope a proposal — but to make it useful, I need [specific info]: budget range, decision timeline, who'll review it. Without those, I'll send something generic that wastes your time. Can we spend 15 min nailing those down first?"

### "We had a bad experience with [similar vendor / similar category]"

Address the wound directly. Don't compare yourself favourably without earning it:

> "That's the reason most teams hesitate on [category] now — the [bad experience] burned a lot of people. Specifically what went wrong with [Vendor X]? I'll tell you straight if we'd have the same problem or not."

This response signals integrity. Sometimes the honest answer is "yes, you'd hit the same wall with us — we're not a fit." That answer is more valuable than winning a deal you'll lose at month 4.

### "Your competitor is offering 50% off"

If the discount is real:

> "Got it. Three questions: Is the discount on the same scope as my proposal? What's their churn / NRR look like for accounts at our profile? And — would you rather have a 50%-off vendor or the one you trust to be there in 18 months?"

Don't match price as a reflex. If you're forced to discount, tie it to a concession (longer contract, expansion commitment, case study participation).

### "We're going to use AI for that instead"

In 2026, this objection appears regularly. Two response patterns:

If your product complements AI:
> "Great direction — most teams I talk to are doing the same. The piece AI doesn't solve yet is [specific gap: structured data integration, compliance, audit trail, X-Y-Z]. That's where we live."

If your product is the AI-replaceable thing:
> "Honest answer: parts of what we do, you CAN replace with an LLM workflow. The parts you can't yet are [specific differentiation: scale, accuracy, compliance, integration]. Worth a 15-min look at the gap?"

Don't claim AI can't do something it can. The prospect will check and you'll lose credibility.

## What NOT to do (universal anti-patterns)

- ❌ Argue or defend ("but actually...") — amplifies the objection
- ❌ Drop the price as the first move on the price objection (cheapens the offer)
- ❌ Promise feature parity you can't deliver ("oh, we'll have that next quarter") — sets you up for churn
- ❌ Use scarcity / urgency manipulation ("the price goes up Monday!") — burns trust
- ❌ Ignore the objection and pitch harder — never works
- ❌ "But Salesforce uses us!" (drop-the-logo) without context — backfires if the prospect is sceptical
- ❌ Reflexive "let me get back to you on that" without actually getting back — kills trust slowly

## Tracking and learning

A vendor with > 20 calls/quarter should track:
- Which objection appears most often (the #1 systemic block)
- Which response pattern produces continuation vs which closes the conversation
- Whether the objection is a real concern or a polite-no signal

Most CRMs let you tag "reason for stalled deal" — actually use that field. A pattern of "too expensive" objections in deals with on-ICP fit usually means the pricing page or the value framing is off, not that the price is too high.

## Reply-branch objections (in cold outbound)

The objection types above also map to reply branches in cold outbound sequences. The patterns are the same; the format is shorter (1–3 sentences in email, vs 1–2 minutes in a live call):

- **Price objection in reply**: "Got it — what budget would make this an obvious yes? We've been honest with founders about which tier fits which stage."
- **Timing objection in reply**: "Makes sense — Q3? Q4? I'll put a placeholder in my calendar and ping you then."
- **Already-using-X in reply**: "Smart — what's the current stack? Most teams switch when [trigger] happens, curious if you're seeing any of that yet."
- **Not-interested in reply**: "All good — taking you off the list. If anything changes, you know where to find me."
